Start From the Problem: Product Intent Over PRDs

Why solution-first PRDs hurt even more when agents write the code, and the short Product Intent doc we use instead

Published Thu, Oct 22, 2026 by Yarel Maman

If you asked me what pushed us forward the most in our AI SDLC, you might be surprised. It’s nothing technical. It’s not Claude Fable, and it’s not AGI.

It was changing the way we define tasks.

The Assembly Line Trap

Long before AI, the lifecycle usually started with a ticket: a list of requirements, or a Product Requirements Document (PRD). An engineer got assigned, and off they went.

Truth is, we got used to thinking of software development as a kind of assembly line, and not entirely fairly. Our own vocabulary doesn’t help: production, staging, the “software factory”.

The big problem with that PRD is that it’s often focused on the desired solution, with a set of rigid requirements. Sometimes it doesn’t describe the customer’s problem at all.

In an ideal world, product and engineering have a two-way interface. Product doesn’t really know the system’s architecture, so engineers are expected to push back: ask why, explain the implications, suggest alternatives. The intent was never “product defines the solution, engineering executes.”

A short conversation between the engineer and the PM might have led to a much simpler solution. One that solves the user’s problem just as well, and saves time and headaches later.

So it’s partly a matter of organizational culture. If you’re in an org where development starts from the solution instead of the problem, ask yourself why, and start challenging it.

Why It Matters Even More in the AI SDLC

Simplifying a bit, you can split software development into tactical and strategic programming:

  • Tactical programming: implementing a plan by writing code.
  • Strategic programming: building it while looking at the whole system and the implications down the road. Am I adding unnecessary complexity? Is there a simpler, cheaper solution? How do I ship this to production with minimal disruption to customers? And most important of all: what problem are we actually trying to solve?

Agents are great at tactical programming. Give an agent a well-defined task and it will execute it with ease. The value engineers bring in this area is shrinking fast.

Strategic programming is something I haven’t yet seen agents do well without an engineer in the loop. That’s where engineers still have a lot of room to make an impact.

Where This Collides With PRDs

Combine solution-first PRDs with agentic development, and you get two main problems:

1. Strategy gets pushed aside. Development drifts toward the tactical, and the strategic gets swept under the rug. Engineers get frustrated, and the odds go up that a few months later you’re stuck with a monster of a system (one they soon won’t recognize) and a big ball of mud.

2. The agents overfit. In the SDLC, it creates a strong overfit toward the tactical. The agents start from a fixed point around the given solution and just head toward it. Even if your SDLC has a brainstorming step, it works poorly when you start from the solution. I learned this the hard way.

What Instead? A Short Product Intent Doc

The concept is simple: focus on the problem. How does it affect the customer? Why is it a problem at all? How would we like the customer to experience the solution? No predefined solution, and no rigid “requirements”.

How do you put it into practice?

  • Write a Skill the PM can run. It interviews them about the problem and ends with the Product Intent doc.
  • The engineer takes it from there. In the SDLC, the engineer moves to strategic programming and brainstorms the problem, which now finally meets the engineering side.
  • Converge together. You land on a solution that’s right and simple, both technically and product-wise.

An hour invested in planning and understanding can save days, even months, of technical mistakes. Simple, but significant. 🎯
